Source × filter = output

The source: F0 and its harmonics

The vocal folds open and close F0 times a second. That buzz holds energy at every whole-number multiple of F0 (the harmonics), each a bit weaker than the last. F0 is what you hear as pitch. Move the F0 slider and watch the harmonic lines spread apart while the filter curve stays put.

The filter: formants

The throat and mouth form a tube with resonances, called formants. Harmonics near a formant come out loud and the rest are damped. F1 rises as the jaw opens and the tongue drops, so it tracks vowel height in reverse. F2 rises as the tongue moves forward, so it tracks frontness.

The third formant

F3 matters less for telling most vowels apart, but it drops with lip rounding and plunges for r-colored vowels. Click /ɝ/ ("heard") and it snaps F3 to about 1690 Hz. Then drag F3 back up to 2500 with F1 and F2 unchanged and the r-quality goes away.

Nasal vowels

Lowering the velum opens a side branch into the nose. That adds a low nasal peak near 250 Hz and an antiresonance (a notch) that climbs through the F1 region, and it widens F1. F2 and F3 barely move. Pick /ɑ/ or /ɛ/, hold the sound on, and slide the velum up for /ɑ̃/ or /ɛ̃/ as in French blanc and vin.

Try this

Pick /i/ ("heed"), then raise F0 to 300 Hz. The harmonics are now so far apart that the lowest one sits above F1, and the filter shape gets sampled only a few times. That is one reason sung vowels from sopranos are hard to identify.

Reference vowels are Peterson & Barney (1952) averages for adult male speakers of American English. Sound is synthesized in your browser: a band-limited sawtooth source passed through a cascade of resonators (Klatt-style), with bandwidths of 70, 100 and 140 Hz and a fixed fourth formant above F3. Nasality adds a peaking and a notching filter (a pole-zero pair, as in Klatt 1980) and widens F1's bandwidth to as much as 200 Hz. It is a simplification of real nasal coupling.
