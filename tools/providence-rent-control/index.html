The Seen and the Unseen:
Rent Control in Providence

Elections have consequences

In April 2026, the Providence City Council passed a rent stabilization ordinance by a vote of 9 to 6. Mayor Brett Smiley vetoed it three days later. The override fell short in May. That would normally be the end of the story, except that David Morales, the state representative who championed the ordinance louder than anyone, is now running for mayor on a promise to bring it back and sign it. Polls have found 72 to 74 percent of residents support capping rent increases, so this is not a fringe position. It may simply be the policy of the next administration.

This essay takes the ordinance seriously and asks the question its supporters and opponents mostly talk past: what do people actually do once the cap is in place? Not what should happen, or what the press release says will happen, but what each person in the housing market, tenant, landlord, would-be landlord, would-be tenant, lender, developer, does when their incentives shift. Economists call these the margins of adjustment.

Everything below is built on the real terms of the 2026 ordinance and actual market facts: 59 percent of Providence households rent, rents are up about 40 percent since 2020 against 15 percent income growth, and a two-bedroom now runs $2,200 to $2,600 depending on whose survey you read. The people are invented, but their situations are drawn from the actual structure of the city's housing stock: old triple-deckers, small landlords, not nearly enough new construction.

The actual terms

Most rent control arguments are about rent control in the abstract. The Providence ordinance is a specific machine with specific parts, and the parts matter. Here is what the version passed in April does. Terms marked with an asterisk come from the introduced text and Council summaries; the final amended ordinance contains further machinery (a fair-return standard, rehabilitation provisions) that press coverage did not detail and this essay does not model.

A 4 percent annual cap, flat
Rent increases capped at 4 percent a year. Not linked to inflation: if CPI runs 6 percent, the cap is still 4.
Vacancy control: one 4% bump at turnover*
When a tenant leaves, the landlord may raise rent 4 percent once for the next tenant. The rent history follows the unit, not the tenant. California and Oregon let rents reset between tenancies; Providence would not. St. Paul tried this and repealed it within 14 months.
Base rent: a 180-day lookback*
The controlled base is the rent charged 180 days before the ordinance takes effect, to prevent last-minute spikes. It also converts every act of past generosity into a permanent ceiling. More on Walt later.
Exempt: owner-occupied buildings of 1–4 units
Exempt if the owner lives on site, plus one additional building of the owner's choosing. The March amendments widened this from three units to four; in a city of triple-deckers it is the largest single carve-out, and a covered building becomes an exempt one the day an owner moves in.
Exempt: new construction, 10 or 20 years
Buildings constructed after the ordinance is law are exempt for 10 years, or 20 if built with prevailing wages and apprenticeship programs. Recently built existing buildings carry a 5-year exemption. Dorms, regulated affordable housing, and future college student housing districts are excluded entirely.
Enforcement: a Rent Board and $250/day fines*
A five-member Rent Board hears petitions and complaints. Landlords can petition for larger increases after major capital improvements; property tax increases above 5 percent pass through by formula. Estimated administration: about $576,000 per year.

How many units would the cap actually cover?

Nobody knows precisely, including the city. Testifying in February, Providence's CFO estimated that fewer than 44 percent of apartments would be rent-controlled, and that was before subtracting the new-construction exemption, which the city cannot compute because it only started tracking new construction three years ago. Here is the arithmetic from public data, with the uncertainty left visible.

Two consequences follow from the denominator. First, the ordinance is narrower than both its champions and its critics talk as if it were: something like a third to a half of Providence's rental units would face the cap, concentrated in non-owner-occupied multifamily buildings owned by landlords like Gil, and in larger buildings owned by LLCs. Second, the city is legislating blind, and so is this essay: Providence has no rental registry, no tracking of renewal increases, and three years of construction data. Every coverage number you see in this debate, including the one above, is estimated.

Chapter 1 · The Seen

The transfer: Marisol and Gil

Start with the effect everyone can see, because it is real and it is the reason the policy polls at 72 percent.

M
Marisol Peña
Tenant · 2nd floor of an Elmwood triple-decker
Medical interpreter, two kids at Roger Williams Middle School. Pays $1,450 for a two-bedroom she's rented since 2021. Market rate for her unit is now about $2,200.
G
Gil Tavares
Landlord · owns two triple-deckers on the same block
Retired RIPTA mechanic, 67. Bought the buildings in the 1990s. Six units total, none owner-occupied, so both buildings are covered. The rents are his pension.

Under the cap, Marisol's rent can rise at most 4 percent a year no matter what Gil would otherwise charge. When his renewal increases would have run above 4 percent, a wedge opens between what she pays and what she would have paid. That wedge is the point of the policy: a transfer from Gil to Marisol, delivered with none of the overhead of a housing voucher. Note what the wedge is not: it is not the full gap between her rent and the asking rent a newcomer sees, because sitting tenants get a discount everywhere, cap or no cap.

Drag the sliders and watch the wedge. Two details the simple story skips: Marisol already pays below asking, as most sitting tenants do, so part of her discount exists with or without the cap; and if the cap shrinks rental supply, the asking rents newcomers face drift higher. Flip on the supply response to see the second one.

Two things are simultaneously true. First: Marisol is better off. At the recent pace of Providence rent increases the cap saves her twenty-odd thousand dollars over a decade, counting only what the cap causes, and she is exactly who the ordinance's sponsors have in mind. Council President Rachel Miller's line, that a rent increase is "an eviction notice by another name," describes a real thing that happens to real families. The best research agrees on this point: in San Francisco, covered tenants were about 20 percent more likely to stay at their address.

Second: Gil is not a corporation. He's a man holding an asset whose income is capped while his costs (insurance, up 30 percent in three years in coastal New England; taxes; a roof) are not. The transfer doesn't come from "landlords" as a class of abstraction. It comes from Gil's pension, one month at a time. What Gil does about that is Chapter 3.

But first: notice the wedge itself creates a new problem. That gap between $1,450 and the market price is now attached to a physical apartment. Whenever it empties, someone gets a prize worth thousands of dollars a year. Who?

Chapter 2 · The Queue

Who gets the $750-a-month prize?

Prices do two jobs. They transfer money, which everyone notices, and they allocate scarce things, which almost nobody does. Cap the price below the market rate and demand exceeds supply. The apartment still gets allocated. Just not by price.

Under the cap, score = (credit − 560)/2.6 + 3 × min(years of history, 10) + min(income, $150k)/3 + noise (±16). Under market pricing, score = 14 × min(income × stretch, $125k) + noise (±160). Stretch is how far each household will go, given its alternatives: Tenaya 2.0 (roommate), Marcus 1.9 (prepaid months; screening rejects him elsewhere, so this unit is worth more to him), Osei 1.2, Erin 1.15, Big Blue 1.0 (fixed corporate budget), Delgado-Kims 0.8 (plenty of alternatives). Highest score wins. The asymmetry being illustrated: money is a margin a newcomer can adjust; credit history is not.

Under market pricing, the unit goes to whoever values it most, and the people who value it most are the ones with the fewest alternatives: Tenaya splits the rent with a roommate, Marcus offers prepaid months because his record fails every screen in town. Under the cap, the price is fixed, so the competition moves entirely to paperwork: credit history, income multiple, references. The landlord, who bears all the downside of a bad tenancy at a rent he can never reset (remember: 4 percent at turnover, once), picks the safest file every time, and the safest file is never a thin one.

A binding price ceiling does not reduce competition for apartments. It changes what the competition is denominated in, from money, which newcomers can offer, to accumulated paperwork, which they cannot. The marginal renter the policy means to help, the one with a real job and a thin file, is the first one sorted out of the queue.

Meanwhile a subtler misallocation compounds. Marisol's neighbors, the Hendersons, raised three kids in their capped three-bedroom. The kids are gone. In a market, the Hendersons would downsize and free the 3BR for a family; under the cap, leaving means surrendering a below-market deal they can never get back, so they stay. Glaeser and Luttmer estimated that about a fifth of New York's controlled apartments are occupied by the "wrong" household in exactly this sense. The cap pays people to sit still, on housing they've outgrown and on jobs they'd otherwise leave: Marisol herself will turn down a better-paying job in Worcester in 2029, because the raise is smaller than the deal she'd forfeit by moving.

Nobody repeals the housing supply by decree. It leaks away, one private, legal, individually reasonable decision at a time, along margins most people never think to watch. The margins, named

1. The maintenance margin. Gil can't raise the rent, but nobody can make him renew the kitchen. A unit is a bundle of rent and upkeep; when the law fixes one, the other adjusts. Massachusetts rent-controlled units were measurably older, shabbier, and less renovated than their neighbors (Sims 2007).

2. The exit margin: condo conversion. A triple-decker is worth more as three condos sold to owner-occupants than as a capped income stream. In San Francisco, buildings subject to rent control saw a 15 percent decline in rental supply, much of it through exactly this door. Gil sells; three renter households' units become two owner households' homes; the block gets richer and the rental stock gets smaller.

3. The exemption margin: owner move-in. Eddie owns the two-family at number 20 and lives in Cranston. The day the cap takes effect, the cheapest renovation available to him is a mattress: move into unit 1, and the building is owner-occupied, 1–4 units, exempt. One rental unit exits the market so that the other can leave the cap. Perfectly legal; the ordinance drew the line, and Eddie walked to it.

4. The platform margin. Karen inherits her mother's triple-decker at number 22. She can become a regulated landlord with a Rent Board file, or she can furnish two units and list them nightly to tourists and traveling nurses, entirely outside the ordinance. Every unit that shifts to short-term rental is a long-term home that no longer exists.

5. The never-built margin. Dev and Priya had an accepted offer on the gut-job three-family at number 24: renovate, rent two units, live in neither. Under the cap their spreadsheet dies (capped income, uncapped renovation costs, no rent reset, ever), so they walk. The house sells to a flipper who makes condos. Three rental units that would have existed, never do. You will not see this on any eviction docket or Rent Board complaint.

6. The generosity margin. Walt, at number 18, has charged his longtime tenants $300 under market for years, because they're good tenants and he's a decent man. The 180-day lookback means his kindness is about to become a legal ceiling, permanently. Every landlord in Providence who reads the ordinance learns the same lesson at the same moment: never price below the maximum again, and get your increase in before the base-rent date. Cities that pass rent control reliably see a burst of pre-effective-date hikes, the law's first observable effect arriving before the law does.

7. The unbundling margin. The 12-unit building on the corner, owned by a Boston LLC, stays rental. But parking is now $150 a month, laundry is app-metered, there's a $95 "amenity and administration fee," and the heat included in the lease becomes tenant-paid utilities at renewal. Rent is capped; the price of renting is not. Regulators everywhere chase this and rarely catch it.

8. The screening margin. Same building: with a queue at every vacancy and no way to price risk, management tightens to 700+ credit, 3.5× income, and documented rental history. Chapter 2, institutionalized.

9. The spillover margin. Rosa and Manny live in their three-family at number 16, so they're exempt. They didn't ask for pricing power, but as covered units leak out of the market, the demand lands on whatever remains. Exempt-sector rents rise faster than they would have. The cap doesn't lower the market price; by shrinking supply it raises it, about 5 percent in the San Francisco study, which is how tenants as a whole can end up paying more in a city that capped the rent.

10. The stayers. And in Marisol's building at number 26, the cap does exactly what it promised: she's still there in 2036, kids through school, savings account no longer empty. Any account of the block keeps her on the ledger too. The question this block poses is never whether Marisol gains. It's how many Tenayas, standing outside a smaller housing stock, paid for it.

Notice three things. First, the asymmetry: at the ordinance's actual terms the cap shaves half a point to a point off the return. That sounds survivable, and for a project that penciled at 9 percent, it is. But development is decided at the margin. The marginal project is the one that penciled at 8.2 against a 7.5 hurdle, and for that one, half a point is the whole ballgame. It dies in a Tuesday investment-committee meeting in Boston, and its 48 units are never missed because they never exist; Cranston and Pawtucket, same workers, same rents, no cap, get the building instead. Second, the labor-linked exemption is a real option with a real price: at a typical prevailing-wage premium, the extra ten years of exemption don't pay for the extra construction cost in this model, so the 20-year track functions more as a labor standard than as developer relief. Third, everything here is one developer's spreadsheet; the model is disclosed so you can disagree with it slider by slider.

You don't have to take the model's word for it. St. Paul ran the experiment.

Chapter 5 · The Literature

What forty years of evidence says

Rent control is among the most-studied policies in urban economics, and the findings are unusually consistent: real protection for incumbents, paid for in supply, quality, and misallocation.

The evidence, on the map
Four natural experiments and the city about to run the fifth. Hover a marker or a card.
"Rent control appears to help affordability in the short run for current tenants, but in the long run decreases affordability, fuels gentrification, and creates negative externalities on the surrounding neighborhood."Rebecca Diamond (Stanford), summarizing the literature, 2018, a researcher whose own work documents rent control's benefits to incumbents

Note what the literature does not say. It does not say rents fall for everyone (they rise for newcomers). It does not say landlords are impoverished as a class (they adapt, exit, or convert). And it does not say incumbent tenants aren't helped (they demonstrably are). The economist's objection to rent control was never "it helps no one." It's that the help is financed by an invisible tax on the city's future tenants. In San Francisco the accounting was almost exactly symmetric: $2.9 billion in gains to covered tenants between 1995 and 2012, and $2.9 billion in costs to the renters who came after them, paid through higher market rents on a smaller stock.

The itemized efficiency losses, with what dollar anchors exist:
  • Withdrawn and never-built supply (the red triangle): units that exit or never arrive. This is the loss that compounds; it took years to accumulate in San Francisco and St. Paul.
  • Misallocation (not in the diagram): the units still supplied go to the wrong households, worth about $400 per covered unit per year in today's dollars (Glaeser & Luttmer), roughly $7M a year on 18,000 units, from day one.
  • Queueing and search costs (the orange gap): time, applications, and side payments spent competing for scarce units. Real, and unmeasured anywhere.
  • Quality depreciation: deferred maintenance is supply withdrawal in slow motion (Sims 2007), so part of the triangle arrives disguised as peeling paint.
For contrast, the blue rectangle, the transfer to sitting tenants, runs roughly $50–60M a year at a $250 monthly wedge. Money changing hands, not money destroyed.

What would change this analysis

The costs above shrink if: the cap rarely binds (market rent growth stays under 4 percent, which is exactly what happened in post-2023 St. Paul, where soft demand, not the ordinance, did the "stabilizing"); the Rent Board runs its pass-throughs generously; or enforcement is light. And the strongest steelman for the ordinance is distributional and temporal: the gains land now, on identifiable struggling households, while the costs land later, diffusely, on people who mostly don't live here yet. A city is allowed to make that trade with open eyes. The argument of this essay is only that the eyes be open: the trade is real, the margins are many, and the bill for Marisol's protection lands on Tenaya.

If the goal is more people affordably housed, experience points to a boring answer: legalize more housing (Providence's stock barely grows), subsidize the poorest directly, and let prices keep doing the one job no board can do for them, telling builders where homes are worth building. The 4 percent cap does something different: it makes the existing shortage more comfortable for the people already housed, while slowly shrinking the stock of homes available to everyone who comes after.
