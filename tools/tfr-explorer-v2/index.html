Situation

Global fertility has collapsed. In the 1960s, the world average was 5 children per woman. By 2023, it had fallen to 2.3—below the long-term replacement rate of 2.1. More than half of all countries (55%+) now sit below replacement. This is unprecedented in human history. Never before have so many people lived in fertility-declining societies simultaneously.

This is not a developed-world story. Bangladesh, Vietnam, Brazil, and Iran have all undergone demographic transitions in a single generation. What took Sweden 150 years has happened in parts of Asia and Latin America in 30.

Complication

But the decline isn't one thing—it's many things. Opportunity cost: as women gain education and enter the workforce, children become more expensive. Secularization removes pro-natalist cultural norms. Marriage itself is declining; in the US, never-married women have fewer children than married women, and the share of never-married adults has tripled since 1970. Income volatility and housing costs make child-rearing less affordable.

The US GSS data makes this visceral: Americans say they want 2.5 children on average (2024), but they have 1.75. Never-married status is the single strongest predictor of lower fertility (standardized coefficient: -0.298). This is not people changing their minds—it's structural barriers preventing them from achieving their desires.

Most alarming: once a country drops below 1.5 TFR, roughly 95% never recover. The fertility trap is real. Low fertility begets more low fertility: smaller cohorts mean fewer births, which means cultural shifts toward smaller families, which means fertility stays low.

Resolution

Understanding the mechanisms matters. The data shows this is primarily a reduction in completed fertility, not merely a delay (women having fewer children across their lifetimes, not just having them later). Early intervention appears more effective than late remediation—policies that address marriage decline or career-childcare conflicts earlier in women's lives may have larger effects than retroactive incentives.

But policy levers exist and effects are modest. No country has reversed a sustained fertility decline through policy alone. South Korea's spending (5% of GDP on family support) has slowed decline but not reversed it. The message isn't hopelessness—it's humility about what policy can do, and focus on understanding what's driving the decline in the first place.

Hypothesis
Opportunity Cost — Women's Education & Employment

As women gain education and career opportunities, the opportunity cost of having children rises, leading to lower fertility.

Key finding: Female education and labor force participation are the 3rd strongest predictors of lower fertility (standardized coefficient: -0.174). However, this is an international pattern. Within the US, education effects are much more modest once marital status is accounted for.
Hypothesis
Marriage Decline as Primary Driver

Declining marriage rates and rising never-married shares explain a large portion of fertility decline in developed nations.

Key finding: Never-married status is the SINGLE STRONGEST predictor of fewer children (standardized coefficient: -0.298). More powerful than education, income, or religion. The rise of never-married adults from 10% (1970s) to 28% (2020s) accounts for a substantial portion of fertility decline.
Hypothesis
Secularization Removes Pro-Natalist Norms

As societies secularize, cultural norms favoring large families weaken, contributing to fertility decline.

Key finding: Religious affiliation shows bimodal distribution: conservative Protestant, Catholic, and Muslim women have substantially more children (often 2.5+ on average), while secular ("None") women have substantially fewer (1.3-1.5). The "None" category has exploded from ~5% in the 1970s to ~28% in 2024, reshaping aggregate fertility.

Key finding: Once a country drops below 1.5 TFR, approximately 95% never recover. Early intervention (before crossing 1.5) is far more cost-effective than attempting recovery.
Hypothesis
J-Curve Hypothesis (Rejected)

Some theorists predicted a "J-curve": fertility falls with early development, then recovers at very high HDI. Data shows monotonic decline instead.

Key finding: There is NO J-curve. The relationship is monotonically negative: higher HDI → lower TFR, consistently across income, life expectancy, and education dimensions. The "recovery at very high development" hypothesis is NOT supported by data.
