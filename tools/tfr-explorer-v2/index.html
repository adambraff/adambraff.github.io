H2: The Housing Affordability Trap

Hypothesis: Rising housing costs relative to income delay household formation and reduce fertility. One study attributes 51% of the U.S. fertility decline to rising housing costs.

Note on data: Cross-country housing affordability data is limited. Our proxy (price-to-income ratio) is only available for 12 countries, making this hypothesis difficult to test rigorously at the international level. The regression results below should be interpreted with caution given the small sample.

H8: Pro-Natalist Policy Effectiveness

Hypothesis: Government spending on family policies (childcare subsidies, parental leave, cash transfers) can moderate fertility decline. But the effects may be modest, with diminishing returns below TFR ~1.5.

Note on interpretation: The family spending coefficient below (0.998) is a fixed-effects regression coefficient, not a correlation. It means: a 1 percentage-point increase in family spending as % of GDP is associated with ~1.0 higher TFR, controlling for country and year fixed effects. This is estimated from only 8 countries with 56 observations, so confidence intervals are wide. The quadratic term suggests diminishing returns.

Machine Learning Model

Approach: A Random Forest model was trained on the panel data to identify which variables best predict TFR across countries and years. Unlike the hypothesis-specific regressions, this model considers all variables simultaneously and can capture non-linear relationships.

What do these variables mean?

Feature Importance (Mean Decrease in Impurity)

How much each variable reduces prediction error when used in the forest's decision splits. Higher = more important for predicting TFR.

Permutation Importance (Model-Agnostic)

How much prediction accuracy drops when each variable's values are randomly shuffled. More robust than impurity-based importance.

Partial Dependence Plots

These show how the model's TFR prediction changes as a single variable moves across its range, averaging over all other variables. The slope indicates the marginal effect: steep = strong influence, flat = weak influence.

Methodology

Data Sources

  • World Bank Open Data: TFR, GDP per capita, female labor force participation, urbanization, internet penetration, mobile subscriptions, education enrollment, life expectancy
  • UN Human Development Reports: HDI proxy (composite of life expectancy, education, income)
  • Economic Policy Uncertainty Index: Baker, Bloom, and Davis global EPU measure
  • OECD Family Database: Family policy spending as % of GDP, parental leave duration
  • General Social Survey (GSS): US-specific data on fertility, marriage, religion, education, income, happiness, and political affiliation from 1972–2022

Statistical Approach

Panel regression with two-way fixed effects (entity + year) is the primary estimation strategy. Entity fixed effects control for time-invariant country characteristics (culture, geography, institutions). Year fixed effects control for global shocks affecting all countries simultaneously. This isolates the within-country, within-year variation to estimate causal effects.

Sample: 265 countries, 2000–2023 (panel unbalanced). Detailed country analysis limited to countries with sufficient data coverage.

Machine Learning: Random Forest with 500 trees trained on the full panel. Feature importance assessed via both mean decrease in impurity and permutation importance. Partial dependence plots show marginal effects.

Key Limitations

  • Cross-country regressions cannot fully establish causation, even with fixed effects
  • Many variables of interest (housing costs, religiosity, policy spending) have limited country coverage
  • GSS data is US-only and may not generalize internationally
  • Time trends in many variables (internet, TFR, urbanization) create confounding that is difficult to fully disentangle
  • The 2000–2023 window may be too short to capture long-run dynamics
